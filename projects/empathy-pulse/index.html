Empathy Pulse

Empathy Pulse is an exploratory wearable interface that translates selected animal behavioral cues into visual, vibrotactile, pneumatic, and audio feedback.

Project Lead: Tingxiao Wu

Wearable Interaction · Embedded Hardware · Pneumatic Feedback

NOTICING CUES ACROSS SPECIES

Animal posture, facial movement, and vocalization can be easy for an untrained observer to miss or misinterpret. This creates a communication gap: a person may want to approach an animal but may not notice cues that suggest comfort, stress, or a need for distance.

The project explores whether bodily feedback can make these cues more noticeable without presenting machine interpretation as certainty.

WHY MULTIMODAL FEEDBACK?

The interaction has two jobs: draw attention to a cue and help the wearer decide how to respond. A screen can explain an interpretation, but it requires a glance. The wearable therefore combines a readable display with bodily feedback that can be noticed peripherally.

The circular display presents the interpreted state and a suggestion. Vibration provides a brief cue, the inflatable bladder adds a changing physical sensation, and audio supplies another feedback channel. The prototype explores these different forms of feedback; it does not establish that all four are necessary or equally effective.

FROM RECOGNITION TESTS TO WEARABLE OUTPUT

An ESP32-CAM captures visual information, while an INMP441 microphone captures nearby sound. A proof-of-concept pipeline processes selected cues and sends an output state to the wearable.

The portfolio documents browser-based recognition explorations using p5.js and a machine-learning library with dog videos, as well as Teachable Machine experiments. These tests explored how selected animal cues could produce output states. The wearable hardware presents those states through the display, vibration motor, pneumatic bladder, and speaker.

APPROACH OR OBSERVE?

Interactive mode explores controlled, close-range encounters with familiar small animals. The scenario emphasizes observation and cautious responses rather than treating a recognition result as permission for direct contact.

Educational mode is designed for more distant encounters. It emphasizes identification and context and can explicitly recommend that the wearer does not approach.

WHAT THE PROTOTYPE CAN CLAIM

The project combines recognition explorations and multimodal wearable prototyping. Its strongest evidence is the design of the input-to-feedback interaction, not validated access to an animal's internal emotional state. General recognition accuracy and the benefit of each feedback channel remain open questions.

A focused next study would compare the display alone with added bodily feedback and examine whether users notice cues without becoming overconfident in the interpretation. Animal-behavior expertise and welfare safeguards would be essential before live-animal testing.
