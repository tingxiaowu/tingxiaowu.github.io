Overview

Empathy Pulse investigates how multimodal feedback might help people notice and respond to animal behavioral cues. The prototype combines camera and microphone input with a circular display, vibration, a small pneumatic bladder, and audio output. Rather than claiming that an animal’s internal emotional state can be determined with certainty, the system uses a demonstrative recognition pipeline to explore how uncertain cues could be translated into legible prompts for safer and more attentive encounters.

Design Challenge

Animal posture, facial movement, and vocalization can be easy for an untrained observer to miss or misinterpret. The wearable therefore prioritizes three principles: make cues noticeable, communicate interpretation cautiously, and distinguish an invitation to interact from a recommendation to keep distance.

System

An ESP32-CAM captures visual information and an INMP441 microphone captures nearby sound. A proof-of-concept pipeline processes selected cues and sends an output state to the wearable. Four feedback channels then translate that state into an embodied prompt.

VisualA 1.28-inch circular display presents a state and a short suggestion.

VibrotactileA vibration motor provides an immediate peripheral cue.

PneumaticA miniature air bladder changes shape to create a slower bodily signal.

AudioA speaker presents an informational or calming sound in the demonstrated scenario.

Interaction Modes

Two scenarios organize the demonstration. In interactive mode, designed for controlled close-range encounters with familiar small animals, the display presents a concise cue and a low-risk response. In educational mode, designed for more distant encounters, the wearable emphasizes identification and context and can recommend that the wearer not approach.

Scope and Reflection

The project produced a working interaction prototype and an end-to-end demonstration with selected animal media, but it did not establish a general-purpose animal-affect classifier. Animal behavior cannot be reduced reliably to a single label from one image or sound, and the wearable should not replace expert interpretation.

A stronger next version would report confidence, fuse temporal posture and vocalization cues, evaluate false positives, and be developed with veterinarians or animal-behavior specialists. It should also test whether the feedback improves human decisions without disturbing animals.
