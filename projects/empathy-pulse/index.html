Empathy Pulse

Empathy Pulse is an exploratory wearable interface that translates selected animal behavioral cues into visual, vibrotactile, pneumatic, and audio feedback.

Project Lead: Tingxiao Wu

Wearable Interaction · Embedded Hardware · Pneumatic Feedback

BACKGROUND

Animal posture, facial movement, and vocalization can be easy for an untrained observer to miss or misinterpret. This creates a communication gap: a person may want to approach an animal but may not notice cues that suggest comfort, stress, or a need for distance.

The project explores whether bodily feedback can make these cues more noticeable without presenting machine interpretation as certainty.

DESIGN CONCEPTION

I designed the experience around three principles: make a cue noticeable, describe its interpretation cautiously, and distinguish an invitation to interact from a recommendation to keep distance.

Rather than relying on a single alert, Empathy Pulse distributes information across four feedback channels. The display communicates the interpreted state, vibration creates an immediate peripheral cue, the inflatable bladder provides a slower bodily signal, and sound adds contextual guidance.

Concept diagram forthcoming

SYSTEM DESIGN

An ESP32-CAM captures visual information, while an INMP441 microphone captures nearby sound. A proof-of-concept pipeline processes selected cues and sends an output state to the wearable.

The output is presented through a 1.28-inch circular display, a vibration motor, a miniature pneumatic bladder, and a speaker. The prototype connects sensing, interpretation, and feedback in one end-to-end demonstration.

INTERACTION MODES

Interactive mode is designed for controlled, close-range encounters with familiar small animals. The wearable presents a concise cue and a low-risk response the wearer can consider.

Educational mode is designed for more distant encounters. It emphasizes identification and context and can explicitly recommend that the wearer does not approach.

PROTOTYPE & REFLECTION

The project produced a functional multimodal prototype and an end-to-end demonstration with selected animal media. It did not establish a general-purpose animal-affect classifier: an animal's state cannot be reliably reduced to one label from a single image or sound.

A future version should report confidence, combine temporal posture and vocalization cues, test false positives, and be developed with veterinarians or animal-behavior specialists. The central evaluation question is whether the feedback supports safer human decisions without disturbing animals.
