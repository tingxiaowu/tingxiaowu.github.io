A narrative VR game about emotional disclosure, privacy, and the boundaries of intimacy.

Overview

Emotion: The Transparent Realm asks what happens when AI can expose feelings people have chosen not to express. Instead of presenting emotion recognition as a neutral convenience, the project treats emotional transparency as a social and ethical conflict. Players move through encounters with a friend, a family member, and a partner, balancing honesty, self-protection, and the expectations that sustain close relationships.

Design Premise

The central question is whether greater emotional transparency necessarily improves a relationship. The game turns this question into an embodied scenario: hidden feelings become visible characters that interrupt a conversation, confront the player, and ultimately reveal the infrastructure responsible for the exposure.

Experience

The game contains three relationship-based levels—friendship, family, and romantic partnership—followed by a final confrontation. In each level, the player encounters a familiar social tension and chooses whether to express a negative feeling or preserve the current relationship dynamic. When the concealed feeling is exposed, emotion figures enter the scene and turn an internal conflict into a spatial challenge.

Tracked controllers support movement, object search, grabbing, comforting gestures, dodging, and the final encounter. The progression deliberately shifts from ordinary conversation toward increasingly visible and uncontrollable disclosure.

Prototype and Formative Feedback

The experience was implemented as a first-person VR prototype with controller-based navigation and object interaction. Development covered NPC orientation, animated emotion figures, shader and lighting work, object collisions, and frame-by-frame effects. Simulator testing exposed inconsistencies in scene tone, perspective tracking, and text occlusion, which were revised before the headset demo.

A formative playtest with three participants suggested that the emotional-transparency premise and embodied interactions were engaging. Participants also noted abrupt transitions between story events and limited audio design. These observations guide iteration; they are not evidence that the experience changes attitudes or relationships.
